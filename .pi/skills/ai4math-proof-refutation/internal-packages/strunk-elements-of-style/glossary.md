# Glossary

**Active voice** — A construction in which the grammatical subject performs the verb's action; Rule 10 generally prefers it for directness and force while allowing useful passives. (Ch 3)

**Antecedent** — The noun or noun phrase to which a pronoun or relative refers. Rule 16 urges placing relatives near their antecedents. (Ch 3)

**Apposition** — A noun or noun phrase placed beside another to identify or explain it; often parenthetic and punctuated accordingly. (Ch 2, 4)

**Clause, coordinate** — An independent clause placed on the same grammatical level as another; a coordinating conjunction normally takes a preceding comma when it introduces the second clause. (Ch 2)

**Clause, independent** — A grammatically complete clause able to stand as a sentence. Two independent clauses without a conjunction should not be joined by a comma. (Ch 2)

**Clause, nonrestrictive** — A relative clause that adds supplementary information without identifying which noun is meant; normally set off by commas. (Ch 2)

**Clause, restrictive** — A clause needed to identify or define its antecedent; normally not set off by commas. (Ch 2)

**Concrete language** — Wording that points to particular people, actions, objects, quantities, or observable details rather than vague abstraction. (Ch 3)

**Dangling modifier** — An introductory modifier whose implied actor does not match the grammatical subject that follows. (Ch 2)

**Emphatic position** — A sentence position used to give prominence; Rule 18 treats the end as the usual strong position for new or climactic material and the beginning as another strong position. (Ch 3)

**Fragment** — A word group punctuated as a sentence although it lacks ordinary sentence completeness; Rule 6 rejects accidental fragments but allows deliberate emphatic ones. (Ch 2)

**Historical prescription** — A rule reported faithfully from the 1918 source whose status may differ in contemporary usage; apply through Appendix A. (Implementation note)

**Loose sentence** — A sentence whose main statement is followed by coordinate or subordinate additions; useful in moderation, monotonous in repeated succession. (Ch 2, 3)

**Modifier proximity** — The principle that modifiers should sit as close as practical to the words they modify to prevent false attachment. (Ch 3)

**Nominalization** — An action expressed primarily as a noun rather than a strong verb; Strunk's active-voice discussion repeatedly favors recasting weak action-noun constructions. (Ch 3)

**Parallel construction** — Similar grammatical form used for ideas of similar content and function. (Ch 3)

**Parenthetic expression** — Material that interrupts the main statement and adds supplementary information; usually enclosed by paired commas. (Ch 2)

**Periodic sentence** — A sentence that delays its main statement or completion, creating suspense and giving the postponed material emphasis. (Ch 3)

**Positive form** — Direct assertion of what is or what happened, rather than evasive wording built around what is not. (Ch 3)

**Serial comma** — The comma before the final conjunction in a series of three or more terms; Strunk's Rule 2 requires it, with a noted business-name exception. (Ch 2)

**Semicolon** — The source's preferred mark between closely related independent clauses not joined by a conjunction; also used before certain adverbial connectors. (Ch 2)

**Topic sentence** — A sentence, usually near the start of an expository or argumentative paragraph, that states the paragraph's governing point. (Ch 3)

**Word economy** — The discipline behind Rule 13: every word and sentence should perform a function; detail may remain when it contributes meaning, evidence, qualification, or rhythm. (Ch 3)
