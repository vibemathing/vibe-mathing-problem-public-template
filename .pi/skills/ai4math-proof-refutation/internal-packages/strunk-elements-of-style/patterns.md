# Patterns

## Clause-Boundary Repair
**When to use**: Two complete clauses are joined by weak or incorrect punctuation.

**How**:
1. Identify the subject and finite verb of each clause.
2. If both clauses can stand alone and there is no conjunction, use a semicolon or period.
3. If a coordinating conjunction joins them, normally place a comma before it.
4. If the relationship is specifically causal, concessive, conditional, or temporal, consider subordination that names that relationship.

**Trade-offs**: Semicolon preserves close relation; period separates; subordination makes dependency explicit. Short parallel or colloquial cases can be exceptions.

## Parenthetic-or-Restrictive Test
**When to use**: A relative clause or inserted phrase may or may not need commas.

**How**:
1. Ask whether the material identifies which person or thing is meant.
2. If identification depends on it, treat it as restrictive and normally omit surrounding commas.
3. If the noun is already identified and the material merely supplements it, treat it as parenthetic and use paired commas.
4. Never insert only one of a required pair.

**Trade-offs**: Punctuation can change meaning, so diagnose function before editing marks.

## Dangling-Modifier Repair
**When to use**: A sentence begins with a participial, adjectival, appositive, or prepositional phrase that seems to modify the wrong subject.

**How**:
1. Name the actor or thing implied by the opening phrase.
2. Check the grammatical subject immediately following it.
3. If they differ, either make the implied actor the subject or recast the opening phrase as a finite clause.
4. Re-read for accidental comic meaning.

**Trade-offs**: Preserve intended focus when choosing the recast.

## Paragraph-Unity Pass
**When to use**: A paragraph feels scattered, overlong, or hard to summarize.

**How**:
1. Write its dominant point in one short sentence.
2. Mark each sentence as statement, explanation, proof, example, implication, transition, or digression.
3. Move or remove material that does not serve the dominant point.
4. Split when a genuinely new topic begins.
5. In exposition or argument, make the purpose visible near the beginning and end on emphasis or consequence.

**Trade-offs**: Narrative, dialogue, and rapid action often need looser paragraph forms.

## Active-Voice Recast
**When to use**: Passive phrasing hides responsibility, weakens a verb, or creates a long action-noun construction.

**How**:
1. Identify the real actor and action.
2. Test an actor–verb–object version.
3. Prefer the active version when it is clearer, shorter, and matches the paragraph's topic.
4. Keep passive when the receiver deserves topic position, the actor is unknown, or naming the actor adds noise.

**Trade-offs**: Blind active conversion can damage information flow; Rule 10 has explicit exceptions.

## Positive-Recast Pass
**When to use**: A sentence tells the reader mainly what did not happen or uses hesitant, noncommittal phrasing.

**How**:
1. Determine the affirmative proposition the writer intends.
2. State that proposition directly with a precise verb or adjective.
3. Retain negation when denial, contrast, or antithesis is itself the point.

**Trade-offs**: Exact negation may be required; precision outranks vigor.

## Concrete-Specificity Pass
**When to use**: The reader gets abstractions, generic praise, or vague categories without a clear picture.

**How**:
1. Ask who or what acted.
2. Replace generic action nouns with observable verbs when possible.
3. Add the significant particulars that let the reader test or imagine the claim.
4. In exposition, pair a general principle with at least one relevant instance when the concept would otherwise remain opaque.

**Trade-offs**: Choose details that carry the point.

## Omit-Needless-Words Pass
**When to use**: A sentence feels padded, indirect, or formulaic.

**How**:
1. Remove throat-clearing and empty frames such as needless `the fact that` constructions.
2. Collapse redundant relatives and action-noun phrases when a direct verb does the job.
3. Combine adjacent sentences that merely reveal one idea step by step.
4. Re-read to restore any qualification, rhythm, or evidence lost in compression.

**Trade-offs**: Concision is functional economy, not minimum word count.

## Sentence-Variety Recovery
**When to use**: A paragraph repeats loose sentences joined by the same connective.

**How**:
1. Highlight repeated `and`, `but`, `so`, or nonrestrictive relatives.
2. Identify the real relations among the thoughts.
3. Recast enough sentences as simple, semicolon-linked, periodic, or differently subordinated structures to represent those relations.
4. Keep some loose sentences when their ease suits the passage.

**Trade-offs**: Structure should follow thought, not cosmetic variety.

## Parallelism Normalization
**When to use**: Lists, paired alternatives, comparisons, or correlative constructions feel uneven.

**How**:
1. Identify items with the same logical function.
2. Choose one grammatical pattern.
3. Align all items to that pattern, including articles or prepositions where needed.
4. For many heterogeneous ideas, group them or use a table instead of forcing one pattern.

**Trade-offs**: Useful repetition can clarify logical equivalence.

## Modifier-Proximity Repair
**When to use**: A modifier could attach to the wrong noun, pronoun, verb, or clause.

**How**:
1. Mark the exact word or group modified.
2. Move the modifier next to that target when practical.
3. Keep subject and principal verb close unless an intentional periodic structure makes the interruption useful.
4. Split the sentence if movement alone cannot remove ambiguity.

**Trade-offs**: Small separations are fine when attachment remains unmistakable.

## Summary-Tense Lock
**When to use**: A summary drifts between present and past or repeatedly announces what the source “says.”

**How**:
1. Choose present or past as the governing tense.
2. Use the relevant perfect form for prior action.
3. Announce once that the passage is a summary, then remove repetitive reporting tags.
4. Keep genuine indirect discourse tense where syntax requires it.

**Trade-offs**: Quotation and chronology may require controlled local tense differences.

## End-Weight Emphasis
**When to use**: A sentence ends on incidental detail while the important claim is buried earlier.

**How**:
1. Identify the new or climactic element.
2. Test moving that material toward the end.
3. Use the beginning for framing or deliberate contrast.
4. Check that the rearrangement does not separate related words or create ambiguity.

**Trade-offs**: Context and meaning also create emphasis.

## Formulaic-Diction Recast
**When to use**: Chapter V flags a word or phrase as hackneyed, vague, redundant, or misused.

**How**:
1. Identify the specific thought the formula is standing in for.
2. Rebuild the clause around that thought, often with a stronger verb or concrete noun.
3. Avoid one-for-one synonym substitution when the whole sentence is structurally vague.
4. Check Appendix A before treating the historical preference as a modern correctness rule.

**Trade-offs**: Some Chapter V judgments are period-specific; the goal is accurate individual thought, not historical imitation unless requested.

## Historical-Applicability Gate
**When to use**: A Strunk rule concerns contested or clearly time-sensitive usage.

**How**:
1. Check Appendix A's classification.
2. For **durable** items, apply normally when context fits.
3. For **style-dependent** items, defer to the user's house style or target publication.
4. For **historical/dated** items, report Strunk's position as historical guidance and do not silently “correct” modern prose to match it.

**Trade-offs**: A user may explicitly want a period imitation or strict Strunk exercise; in that mode, historical prescriptions become intentional constraints.
