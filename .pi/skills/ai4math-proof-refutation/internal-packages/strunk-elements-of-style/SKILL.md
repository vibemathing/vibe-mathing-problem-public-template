## Operational Editing Workflow

### 1. Classify the request

Choose one route before editing:

| Request | Route |
|---|---|
| Fix grammar or punctuation | Usage route → ch02 |
| Make prose clearer or tighter | Composition route → ch03 |
| Improve a paragraph or argument | Paragraph/composition route → ch03 |
| Check a word, idiom, or disputed construction | Diction route → ch05 + Appendix A |
| Check formatting conventions | Form route → ch04 |
| Ask what Strunk historically prescribed | Historical-reference route → relevant chapter; report it as historical guidance |
| Practice or evaluate mastery | Exercise route → ch07 |

If the task is unrelated to English prose or Strunk's rules, do not invoke this skill.

### 2. Establish constraints

Infer intended meaning, audience, tone, genre, and house style. Apply this precedence:

1. Preserve intended meaning and factual content.
2. Respect explicit user or house-style constraints when they are grammatically viable.
3. Apply durable structural principles from Chapters II–III.
4. Apply historically contingent preferences only when the user asks for Strunk's historical style or the convention still matches the target context.

### 3. Diagnose before rewriting

Inspect in this order:

1. **Sentence integrity** — fragments, comma splices, clause boundaries, dangling introductory phrases.
2. **Relationships** — restrictive vs. parenthetic material, modifier attachment, coordination, parallel form.
3. **Paragraph logic** — one dominant topic, clear opening purpose, development, useful ending.
4. **Directness** — active voice where it improves force, positive statements, concrete and specific wording.
5. **Economy** — remove words and sentences that add no function; combine needless step-by-step statements.
6. **Rhythm and emphasis** — vary repetitive loose structures and place the intended emphasis deliberately.
7. **Form and spelling** — check only conventions relevant to the task.
8. **Applicability** — consult Appendix A before enforcing a historically dated usage rule.

### 4. Choose the smallest effective intervention

- **Minimal edit**: repair the local defect while preserving the user's wording and cadence.
- **Structural edit**: recast a sentence or paragraph when local substitution would leave the underlying relation unclear.
- **Full rewrite**: use only when the user asks for it or when the original structure prevents a reliable local repair.

When a ready-made formula distorts the thought, recast the sentence instead of swapping synonyms.

### 5. Explain decisions when useful

For material edits, name the principle and effect when explanation is useful.

### 6. Validate the result

Run the SELF_CHECK. If a correction creates ambiguity, flattens an intentional rhetorical effect, or conflicts with the target style, revise again.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

The numbered names preserve Strunk's formulations; routing is operational synthesis.

### Sentence integrity and punctuation

- **Rule 3 — Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas.** Use when a word, phrase, or nonrestrictive clause interrupts the main statement. Determine whether the material merely adds information or identifies the noun; restrictive material normally stays unbracketed.
- **Rule 4 — Place a comma before a conjunction introducing a co-ordinate clause.** Use when two independent clauses are joined by a coordinating conjunction. If the relation is more specific than simple coordination, consider subordination that names cause, contrast, or condition.
- **Rule 5 — Do not join independent clauses by a comma.** For closely related complete clauses without a conjunction, use a semicolon or separate sentences; short parallel or colloquial cases are source-noted exceptions.
- **Rule 6 — Do not break sentences in two.** Repair accidental fragments. Preserve an isolated word or phrase when its rhetorical emphasis is deliberate and unmistakable.
- **Rule 7 — A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject.** The actor implied by the opener must be the grammatical subject that follows.

### Paragraph architecture

- **Rule 8 — Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic.** Treat a paragraph break as a signal that the development has taken a new step. Split a paragraph when it carries distinct topics; combine when separation obscures one continuous point.
- **Rule 9 — As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning.** In exposition and argument, reveal the purpose early, develop it, and end on emphasis or consequence. Narrative and dialogue may be looser.

### Direct, concrete, economical prose

- **Rule 10 — Use the active voice.** Prefer active constructions when they are more direct, vigorous, and concise. Retain passive voice when the receiver of the action is the paragraph's proper topic or when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Avoid stacked passives and action-noun subjects paired with weak verbs.
- **Rule 11 — Put statements in positive form.** State the claim directly; keep negation for genuine denial or strong antithesis.
- **Rule 12 — Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Replace vague abstractions with significant particulars; connect general claims to useful instances.
- **Rule 13 — Omit needless words.** Make every word carry a function. Cut padding and redundancy; combine stepwise statements when they express one idea. Preserve useful detail.

### Structure, relation, and emphasis

- **Rule 14 — Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** A loose sentence may be natural; repeated identical connective patterns become mechanical. Vary structure to match thought.
- **Rule 15 — Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form.** Parallel meaning calls for parallel grammar in lists, correlatives, and successive points.
- **Rule 16 — Keep related words together.** Keep modifiers near targets, relatives near antecedents, and subject near principal verb when practical.
- **Rule 17 — In summaries, keep to one tense.** Choose a time frame and hold it; use perfect forms for prior action and avoid repetitive reporting tags.
- **Rule 18 — Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** Put new or climactic material late when that serves the intended emphasis; use order deliberately.

### Book-wide operating principle

The Introduction presents these rules as a compact set of essentials, not a complete theory of English style. It also recognizes that skilled writers may violate a rule when the violation buys a compensating effect. When a user's deliberate construction clearly earns its effect, explain the trade-off instead of flattening it into uniform correctness.

## Decision Logic

| Signal | Primary action | Fallback / exception |
|---|---|---|
| Two independent clauses separated only by comma | Apply Rule 5: semicolon, period, or conjunction + comma | Allow very short parallel clauses or deliberate colloquial dialogue when the effect is clear |
| Introductory participial phrase points to a different actor than the subject | Apply Rule 7: change subject or recast phrase | If attachment is genuinely ambiguous, offer alternative readings before rewriting |
| Paragraph contains two independent topics | Apply Rule 8: split by topic | Keep together if the second topic is actually evidence or consequence of the first |
| Passive hides the actor or creates weak nominal phrasing | Apply Rule 10: test an active recast | Keep passive when the receiver is the intended topic or actor is unknown/irrelevant |
| Sentence relies on vague abstraction or generic approval words | Apply Rule 12: ask what happened, who acted, what changed | Preserve abstraction when the domain genuinely requires a general category, then add an example if useful |
| Sentence is padded with formulaic phrases | Apply Rule 13: delete or recast | Retain detail that contributes meaning, rhythm, qualification, or evidence |
| Series/list mixes grammatical shapes | Apply Rule 15: normalize parallel form | Group heterogeneous ideas or use a table when one parallel pattern would distort them |
| Modifier could attach to the wrong noun | Apply Rule 16: move it next to its target or split sentence | Preserve deliberate periodic delay only if ambiguity remains low |
| Usage rule comes from Chapter V or VI and is historically contingent | Check Appendix A before editing | Report the historical rule without enforcing it in modern prose unless requested |

## Failure Recovery

- **Meaning is unclear**: do not guess a single rewrite. State the ambiguity and give the smallest set of plausible readings or edits.
- **A rule conflicts with explicit house style**: follow the house style when the result remains clear and grammatical; identify the difference only if relevant.
- **A fragment or passive is intentional**: preserve it when it creates a justified rhetorical effect and readers can parse it reliably.
- **A local edit fails**: recast the sentence around its actual logical relation rather than stacking patches.
- **A historical prescription conflicts with current usage**: label it as Strunk's historical preference and use the modern-applicability route.
- **Several rules point in different directions**: prioritize meaning, clause relationship, and modifier attachment before concision or rhythm.
- **The source rule does not settle the case**: say that the book is insufficient for a definitive judgment and avoid inventing an authorial position.

## SELF_CHECK

Before returning an edit or rule judgment, verify:

- I understand the user's intended meaning, audience, and requested degree of intervention.
- I chose the relevant chapter and rule instead of applying a generic style preference.
- The rule's prerequisites fit the sentence or paragraph in front of me.
- I preserved facts, logical relationships, tone, and deliberate rhetorical effects unless the user asked to change them.
- I checked exceptions noted by the source, especially for passive voice, fragments, loose sentences, and paragraph forms.
- I did not silently enforce a dated Chapter V or VI prescription in modern prose.
- My revision improves at least one of correctness, clarity, force, economy, relation, or emphasis without introducing a new defect.
- Parallel items remain parallel; modifiers attach cleanly; tense is controlled; punctuation reflects clause structure.
- The final paragraph still has a discernible purpose and ending.
- If the case remains uncertain, I say what information or convention would settle it.

## Scope & Limits

This skill operationalizes the 1918 original edition of *The Elements of Style* by William Strunk Jr. It does not incorporate E. B. White's later revisions. It is strongest on plain-English sentence and paragraph editing. The source predates many modern usage conventions, so Chapter V and VI prescriptions must pass the applicability gate before they are used as present-day authority. For publication-specific punctuation, citation systems, inclusive-language conventions, or a named house style, combine this skill with the relevant current guide.
