# Chapter 3: Elementary Principles of Composition

## Core Idea

Composition improves when the writer controls units and relationships: one paragraph develops one topic; sentences make their subjects, actions, modifiers, coordinate ideas, tense, and emphasis easy to follow; wording stays direct, positive, concrete, and economical.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Rule 8 — Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic

- **When to use**: planning or revising multi-sentence prose.
- **How**: identify the topic each paragraph develops. Start a new paragraph when the development takes a new step. Very short treatments may need only one paragraph; longer subjects should be subdivided by meaningful topics.
- **Exceptions**: transitions, textbooks, guidebooks, and dialogue may legitimately use single-sentence paragraphs more often.

### Rule 9 — As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning

- **When to use**: exposition and argument especially.
- **How**: make the paragraph's governing statement visible near the start; use the middle to explain, define, exemplify, prove, or develop it; finish with emphasis or consequence that fits the opening.
- **Failure signal**: a paragraph ends with a digression or minor detail and leaves its main point unresolved.
- **Exceptions**: narrative and description may use openings that simply orient the reader, and rapid narrative may omit an explicit topic sentence.

### Rule 10 — Use the active voice

- **When to use**: passive phrasing is indirect, wordy, vague about the actor, or built around a weak action noun.
- **How**: identify the actor and action; test a direct active verb.
- **Exception**: passive voice is useful when the receiver of the action is the paragraph's natural topic or the actor is unknown or unimportant.
- **Failure signal**: one passive construction depends on another, or a noun naming the whole action becomes subject while the verb contributes little.

### Rule 11 — Put statements in positive form

- **When to use**: wording is hesitant, evasive, or defined mainly by what something is not.
- **How**: state the intended positive fact with a precise verb, noun, or adjective.
- **Exception**: negation is strong when denial or antithesis is itself the point.

### Rule 12 — Use definite, specific, concrete language

- **When to use**: claims are vague, abstract, generic, or hard to picture.
- **How**: replace broad categories with significant particulars; name actors, actions, quantities, objects, or examples that allow the reader to form a clear image or test the claim.
- **Reasoning**: even abstract exposition benefits from concrete instances of the principle in use.

### Rule 13 — Omit needless words

- **When to use**: a sentence or paragraph contains formulaic frames, redundant relative constructions, weak nominal phrases, or a step-by-step sequence that expresses one idea.
- **How**: delete words and sentences that perform no function; use direct verbs; combine where combination makes the real relation clearer.
- **Boundary**: concision does not demand short sentences, missing detail, or skeletal treatment. Significant detail remains.

### Rule 14 — Avoid a succession of loose sentences

- **When to use**: a paragraph repeatedly uses the same coordinate pattern, often with `and`, `but`, `so`, `which`, `when`, or `while`.
- **How**: recast enough sentences as simple, semicolon-linked, periodic, or differently subordinated constructions to represent the actual relationships among thoughts.
- **Boundary**: an occasional loose sentence is natural and can relieve overly formal prose.

### Rule 15 — Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form

- **When to use**: lists, paired alternatives, comparisons, correlative constructions, and repeated points.
- **How**: give ideas of equal function matching grammatical form; keep articles or prepositions consistently shared or consistently repeated; align the elements after `both/and`, `either/or`, `not/but`, and similar pairs.
- **Scale tactic**: if many ideas cannot share one pattern naturally, classify them into groups or use a table.

### Rule 16 — Keep related words together

- **When to use**: a sentence allows a modifier, relative clause, or adverb to attach to the wrong word.
- **How**: place modifiers close to their targets, relatives close to their antecedents, and avoid needless material between subject and principal verb.
- **Exception**: deliberate periodic structure may separate related elements to create suspense if the relation stays clear.

### Rule 17 — In summaries, keep to one tense

- **When to use**: summarizing narrative, drama, essays, speeches, or source material.
- **How**: choose a governing tense and maintain it; use the relevant perfect for events prior to the summary's main time frame. Establish once that the passage is a summary instead of repeatedly adding reporting formulas.
- **Boundary**: indirect discourse and genuine chronology may require local tense distinctions.

### Rule 18 — Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end

- **When to use**: the most important or newest material is buried before incidental detail.
- **How**: test a version that places the climactic element at the end. Use the beginning for another deliberate point of emphasis or framing.
- **Scale**: the same principle applies to sentence endings, paragraph endings, and larger composition endings.

## Key Concepts

- **Paragraph unity**: one governing topic per paragraph.
- **Topic sentence**: an early statement of the paragraph's purpose.
- **Active voice**: subject performs the action.
- **Positive form**: direct assertion of what is or happened.
- **Concrete diction**: particular, definite terms that evoke a specific object, event, or instance.
- **Concision**: removing material that adds no function without removing useful detail.
- **Loose sentence**: main clause followed by coordinate or subordinate additions.
- **Parallel construction**: similar form for similar logical function.
- **Modifier proximity**: placing related elements near one another.
- **Tense control**: a deliberate, consistent time frame.
- **End weight**: placing important material in the strong final position.

## Mental Models

- **Paragraphs are development units**: a paragraph break tells the reader that the argument has taken a new step.
- **Strong prose aligns grammar with thought**: actors tend toward subjects, actions toward verbs, equal ideas toward equal forms, modifiers toward their targets, and new information toward strong positions.
- **Compression should reveal structure**: when cutting words exposes the real actor, action, or relation, concision and clarity reinforce each other.
- **Variety follows logic**: change sentence form because relations differ, not because repetition itself feels cosmetically undesirable.

## Anti-patterns

- **Paragraph dumping**: collecting several topics under one block because they are generally related.
- **Passive prohibition**: converting every passive even when it preserves the right topical focus.
- **Negative fog**: using `not` plus weak qualifiers when a precise positive term exists.
- **Abstract praise**: calling something “interesting,” “important,” or “effective” without showing the concrete basis.
- **Shortest-is-best editing**: cutting useful qualification or evidence merely to reduce word count.
- **Mechanical sentence variety**: changing grammar randomly instead of representing logical relations.
- **False parallelism**: aligning the surface form of ideas that do not have the same logical function.
- **Floating modifiers**: allowing `only`, relative clauses, or prepositional phrases to sit where they can modify more than one target.
- **Tense drift**: switching time frames without a chronological or discourse reason.
- **Weak landing**: ending the sentence or paragraph on incidental information.

## Worked Example

Draft paragraph:

> There were a number of customer reports that were received by the support team, and the reports were not very specific, and an investigation of the issue was then carried out by engineering. The fact that logs were incomplete was a factor in making the investigation difficult. There were several attempts that were made to reproduce the failure, and they did not have success.

Operational pass:

1. **Rule 8/9**: the paragraph has one topic—why diagnosis was difficult—so keep it together and make that purpose explicit.
2. **Rule 10**: replace passive and action-noun constructions with actors and verbs.
3. **Rule 11**: replace vague negatives with direct statements.
4. **Rule 12**: name what was missing.
5. **Rule 13**: remove `there were`, `the fact that`, and `was a factor in` frames.
6. **Rule 14**: avoid three loose `and` chains.
7. **Rule 18**: land on the unresolved outcome.

Revision:

> Sparse customer reports gave support little to reproduce. Engineering investigated, but the reports omitted timestamps and the available logs were incomplete. After several reproduction attempts, the failure remained unresolved.

This version is shorter because its grammar exposes the actual actors, actions, evidence gap, and outcome. If “timestamps” were not known from the source material, replace that detail with the actual missing information rather than inventing specificity.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make paragraph boundaries represent topic boundaries.
2. Let grammar expose the real actor, action, logical equality, and modifier relation.
3. Prefer active, positive, concrete, economical wording when it improves force and clarity.
4. Preserve passive voice, fragments, and looser structures when they serve a clear function.
5. Vary sentence structure in response to differing logical relationships.
6. Keep a deliberate tense system in summaries.
7. Use sentence and paragraph endings as intentional positions of emphasis.
8. Never buy concision with lost evidence or altered meaning.
