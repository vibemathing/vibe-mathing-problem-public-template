# Chapter 5: Words and Expressions Commonly Misused

## Core Idea

This chapter is a diagnostic catalog of expressions Strunk considered wrong, disputed, vague, hackneyed, redundant, or frequently misapplied. Its most reusable method appears in the introduction: when a ready-made phrase has displaced exact thought, reconstruct the sentence around the intended meaning instead of swapping one stock phrase for another.

Many individual prescriptions reflect 1918 usage. For modern editing, apply the **recast-the-thought** method broadly and send individual usage judgments through Appendix A.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Classify the problem before correcting

The source distinguishes several kinds of entries:

- forms treated as direct errors;
- disputed constructions the period's careful writers often avoided;
- ordinary words weakened by overuse or vagueness;
- formulas that save the writer from expressing an individual thought precisely.

**Operational rule**: decide whether the problem is grammar, semantics, register, cliché, concision, or historical preference. Do not report all six with the same severity.

### 2. Recast instead of patching

- **When to use**: an expression such as `factor`, `feature`, `case`, `character`, `nature`, `system`, or `interesting` carries little exact meaning.
- **How**:
  1. identify the concrete fact or relation the writer means;
  2. identify the actor and action;
  3. rebuild the clause around that meaning;
  4. delete the formula if it contributes nothing.
- **Failure signal**: a one-for-one synonym replacement leaves the sentence equally vague.

### 3. Preserve real semantic distinctions

Some entries are valuable because they protect meaning rather than enforce fashion. Examples include the source's discussions of `affect/effect`, `divided into/composed of`, factual claims versus judgments, relative-clause agreement, and the grammatical role of `who/whom` in embedded clauses.

**Operational rule**: when a word choice changes the proposition, fix the semantic relation first. Style preferences come later.

### 4. Apply a historical gate

The chapter contains several prescriptions that should not become silent contemporary autocorrections, including the source's treatment of singular `they`, split infinitives, `shall/will`, `data`, `due to`, `different than`, and other period-sensitive forms.

**Operational rule**: load Appendix A before treating a Chapter V entry as present-day authority.

## Key Concepts

- **Ready-made formula**: familiar wording that can conceal the writer's actual thought.
- **Hackneyed word**: an expression weakened by habitual, generic use.
- **Semantic distinction**: a difference in meaning that a near-synonym substitution can erase.
- **Register**: the level of formality and convention appropriate to a genre or audience.
- **Historical prescription**: a source rule tied to the usage norms of its period and requiring a modern applicability check.
- **Recast**: rebuild the sentence around the intended meaning instead of patching one word.

## Mental Models

- **Ask what the formula is hiding**: when `factor`, `feature`, `case`, or `interesting` appears, identify the specific cause, action, property, or evidence the writer means.
- **Separate correctness from freshness**: some entries involve grammar or semantics; others involve dullness or overuse.
- **Date the lexicon**: the chapter reveals Strunk's editorial instincts, yet its individual bans do not all survive as current standard-English rules.

## Anti-patterns

- **Historical over-enforcement**: “correcting” singular `they`, every split infinitive, or every adverbial `due to` merely because this edition objects.
- **Synonym whack-a-mole**: replacing one vague cliché with another while leaving the thought unexamined.
- **False certainty**: presenting a register preference as a grammar error.
- **Vocabulary deletion without semantic repair**: removing a word that carried a needed distinction and leaving the sentence less precise.

## Worked Example

Draft:

> An important factor in the success of the rollout was the fact that the support team was very responsive, and an interesting feature of the process was that issues were fixed quickly.

A surface substitution would merely replace `factor` and `feature` with different abstract nouns. Recast the thought:

> The rollout succeeded in part because support answered quickly and resolved issues before they spread.

What changed:

- `factor` became an explicit causal relation;
- `the fact that` disappeared;
- `very responsive` became observable behavior;
- `interesting feature` disappeared because the sentence now states the information;
- `were fixed` became a direct action with an actor.

If the evidence does not support “before they spread,” omit that detail. Specificity must come from known facts.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use Chapter V as a diagnostic library plus a method for recasting formulaic prose.
2. Classify the issue before choosing the severity of correction.
3. Strong fixes often rebuild the sentence around the actual thought.
4. Preserve semantic distinctions where they determine meaning.
5. Route period-specific bans through Appendix A and label historical advice.
6. Load only the relevant alphabetic reference to keep context small.
