# Chapter 6: Spelling

## Core Idea

The chapter treats spelling as a convention sustained by general agreement and argues that conspicuously nonstandard spellings impose needless attention costs on readers. Its broad reader-oriented principle remains useful; several concrete spellings in the 1918 edition have since changed and must be treated historically.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Follow established reader expectation

- **When to use**: deciding between standard and idiosyncratic spellings in ordinary prose.
- **How**: prefer the form readers recognize automatically unless the genre, dialect, quotation, brand, or historical voice gives a reason to depart.
- **Why**: unfamiliar simplifications can make the reader pause to reconstruct a familiar word, consuming attention without adding meaning.

### Consonant doubling before `-ed` and `-ing`

The source states a practical rule: a single consonant other than `v`, preceded by a stressed short vowel, is doubled before `-ed` and `-ing`, with examples such as forms equivalent to *planned*, *letting*, and *beginning* and an exception noted for *coming*.

Use a current dictionary for edge cases, dialect differences, and present-day standard forms.

### Historical compound and hyphenation forms

The source instructs writers to use hyphenated forms corresponding to `to-day`, `to-night`, and `to-morrow`, and separate forms such as `any one`, `every one`, `some one`, and some uses of `some time`.

**Modern route**: these particular recommendations are historical evidence, not default present-day spellings. Modern `today`, `tonight`, and `tomorrow` should not be “corrected” to the source's hyphenated forms in ordinary contemporary prose.

## Key Concepts

- **Conventional spelling**: the form established by broad reader and editorial practice.
- **Attention cost**: processing effort created when an unusual form forces the reader to decode what would otherwise be automatic.
- **Morphological spelling rule**: a spelling change triggered by adding an inflection such as `-ed` or `-ing`.
- **Historical spelling**: a form normal or preferred in the source period that may no longer be current.

## Mental Models

- **Spelling is interface design for reading**: a standard form lets the reader spend attention on meaning rather than decoding.
- **Date the rule before enforcing it**: spelling is explicitly described in the chapter as convention that changes through general agreement; therefore a century-old specific form requires current verification.

## Anti-patterns

- **Historical regression**: changing contemporary `today` to `to-day` because the 1918 source recommends it.
- **Innovation for its own sake**: using unconventional simplifications that make readers reconstruct ordinary words.
- **Overgeneralizing a morphological rule**: applying consonant doubling without considering stress, dialect, and dictionary-standard exceptions.

## Worked Example

Task: review modern product copy containing `tomorrow`.

Historical source rule: the chapter prescribes the hyphenated form used in its period.

Modern editing action: keep `tomorrow` unless the user asks for a historical imitation. If the question is “What would Strunk's 1918 edition prescribe?”, report the historical spelling and label the time context.

This preserves both source fidelity and modern usefulness.

## Key Takeaways

1. The chapter's durable principle is reader familiarity: spelling should minimize needless decoding effort.
2. Consonant-doubling logic remains useful but should be checked against current standard spelling.
3. Several specific forms in the chapter are dated.
4. Never use the historical spelling list as a silent modern autocorrect dictionary.
