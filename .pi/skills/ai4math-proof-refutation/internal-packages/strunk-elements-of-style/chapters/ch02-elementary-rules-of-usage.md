# Chapter 2: Elementary Rules of Usage

## Core Idea

Seven rules handle common problems of possession, series punctuation, parenthetic material, clause boundaries, sentence fragments, and dangling introductory modifiers. The underlying discipline is structural: punctuation should represent grammatical relationships, and introductory modifiers should attach to the subject they describe.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Rule 1 — Form the possessive singular of nouns by adding `'s`

- **When to use**: singular possessive nouns.
- **How**: add `'s` even when the noun ends in a consonant sound such as *s*; the source lists historical-name and fixed-expression exceptions.
- **Boundary**: pronominal possessives such as `hers`, `its`, `theirs`, and `yours` take no apostrophe.

### Rule 2 — In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last

- **When to use**: a three-or-more-item series joined by one final `and` or `or`.
- **How**: separate each item by a comma, including the comma before the conjunction.
- **Exception in source**: some business names omit the final comma.
- **Modern routing**: treat this as a house-style choice when a publication explicitly follows another convention; see Appendix A.

### Rule 3 — Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas

- **When to use**: a word, phrase, appositive, or nonrestrictive clause adds supplementary information without identifying its antecedent.
- **How**: place matching punctuation around the inserted material. If interruption is slight, commas may sometimes be omitted, but do not use only one of a required pair.
- **Decision point**: a relative clause that identifies which person or thing is meant is restrictive and normally stays unbracketed; a clause about an already identified noun is supplementary and normally takes commas.
- **Special source cases**: dates, abbreviations such as `etc.` and `jr.`, and dependent phrases can function parenthetically.

### Rule 4 — Place a comma before a conjunction introducing a co-ordinate clause

- **When to use**: a coordinating conjunction such as `and` or `but` introduces another independent clause.
- **How**: place the comma before the conjunction.
- **Reasoning step**: if the relation is really cause, contrast, concession, or condition, a subordinate construction may express the logic more exactly than generic coordination.
- **Exception**: where a shared subject is stated only once, the source distinguishes cases such as close `and` coordination from `but`; diagnose the actual clause structure before inserting punctuation.

### Rule 5 — Do not join independent clauses by a comma

- **When to use**: two grammatically complete clauses appear in one sentence without a coordinating conjunction.
- **How**: use a semicolon when the relation is close, or split into sentences. With a coordinating conjunction, use a comma before the conjunction.
- **Adverb warning**: conjunctive adverbs such as `therefore` do not by themselves license a comma splice.
- **Source exceptions**: very short clauses with parallel form can sometimes take a comma; colloquial dialogue can also depart from the formal default.

### Rule 6 — Do not break sentences in two

- **When to use**: a dependent phrase or clause has been punctuated as a separate sentence without a rhetorical reason.
- **How**: join it to the sentence it completes or rewrite it as an independent sentence.
- **Exception**: an isolated word or expression may stand as a sentence for warranted emphasis when the construction is unmistakably deliberate.

### Rule 7 — A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject

- **When to use**: an opening participial phrase—and, by extension in the chapter, similar introductory adjectival, appositive, or prepositional material—implies an actor or referent.
- **How**: make that actor or referent the grammatical subject that follows. If another subject is needed, recast the introduction as a finite clause or relocate it.
- **Failure signal**: the opening phrase accidentally describes an inanimate object or a different person and produces an absurd reading.

## Key Concepts

- **Parenthetic expression**: supplementary material that interrupts the main statement.
- **Restrictive clause**: a clause required to identify the antecedent.
- **Nonrestrictive clause**: supplementary information about an already identified antecedent.
- **Coordinate clause**: an independent clause at the same grammatical level as another.
- **Comma splice**: independent clauses joined only by a comma.
- **Fragment**: an incomplete construction punctuated as a full sentence without justified rhetorical purpose.
- **Dangling modifier**: an introductory modifier whose implied actor does not match the grammatical subject.

## Mental Models

- **Punctuation follows structure**: determine whether units are independent, subordinate, restrictive, or parenthetic before choosing the mark.
- **Commas come in functional pairs**: when an insertion genuinely interrupts a sentence, test whether removing the inserted material leaves a complete main statement.
- **The subject owns the opener**: mentally expand an introductory participial phrase into “while/when SUBJECT was …”; if the supplied subject does not fit, the modifier dangles.

## Anti-patterns

- **Surface comma insertion**: adding commas by pause or rhythm alone without checking clause function.
- **Single-sided parenthesis**: inserting one comma around an interrupting phrase and forgetting the second.
- **Conjunctive-adverb comma splice**: writing `Clause, therefore clause` when both clauses are independent.
- **Fragment camouflage**: assuming capitalization and a period make an incomplete group a sentence.
- **Actor mismatch**: beginning with an action phrase and following it with a subject that cannot perform the action.

## Worked Example

Draft:

> After reviewing the incident report, the root cause was obvious, therefore the team changed the retry policy.

Diagnosis:

1. `After reviewing the incident report` implies a reviewer, yet the grammatical subject is `the root cause`; Rule 7 fails.
2. `the root cause was obvious` and `the team changed the retry policy` are independent clauses; `therefore` is an adverb, so the comma creates the Rule 5 problem.

One repair:

> After reviewing the incident report, the team saw the root cause; therefore, it changed the retry policy.

A more explicit causal version:

> After reviewing the incident report, the team identified the root cause and changed the retry policy accordingly.

Choose between them based on whether the causal sequence or the close relation between two assertions deserves emphasis.

## Key Takeaways

1. Classify clause and modifier relationships before editing punctuation.
2. Use paired commas for genuinely parenthetic material; keep identifying clauses attached without those commas.
3. A coordinating conjunction and a conjunctive adverb have different punctuation behavior.
4. Repair accidental fragments, while allowing deliberate rhetorical fragments when the effect is unmistakable.
5. Make the grammatical subject match the actor implied by an opening phrase.
6. The serial comma is part of Strunk's rule set; modern house style may control that preference.
