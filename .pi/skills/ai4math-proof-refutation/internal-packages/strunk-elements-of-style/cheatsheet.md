# Cheatsheet

## Fast Routing

| If you see… | Ask… | Usually do… | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comma between two complete clauses | Can each side stand alone? | Semicolon/period, or conjunction + comma | R4–5 |
| Inserted clause in commas | Does it merely add information? | Commas for parenthetic/nonrestrictive; no commas when identifying | R3 |
| Opening participial phrase | Who performs that action? | Make that actor the grammatical subject | R7 |
| Paragraph with two centers of gravity | Can one topic sentence cover all sentences? | Split by topic or remove digression | R8–9 |
| Hidden actor / action noun + weak verb | Would an active verb sharpen it? | Test active recast; keep useful passive | R10 |
| “not very…”, evasive negative | What positive claim is intended? | State the affirmative directly | R11 |
| Vague abstract wording | What happened, specifically? | Add concrete nouns, verbs, significant details | R12 |
| Padding / `the fact that` / redundant frames | Does each word add function? | Delete or recast | R13 |
| Many sentences built `…, and …` / `…, but …` | Are the relations really identical? | Vary structure to match thought | R14 |
| List items have mixed grammar | Are they coordinate ideas? | Make forms parallel | R15 |
| Modifier sits far from target | What exactly does it modify? | Move it next to target or split sentence | R16 |
| Summary changes tense | What is the governing time frame? | Choose one tense system and hold it | R17 |
| Important point appears mid-sentence | What should land hardest? | Test moving it to the end | R18 |

## Editing Order

1. Meaning and audience.
2. Sentence completeness and clause boundaries.
3. Attachment, coordination, and parallelism.
4. Paragraph unity and development.
5. Active/positive/concrete wording.
6. Concision.
7. Rhythm and emphasis.
8. Form/spelling.
9. Historical-applicability check.

## Smells

- comma splice
- unmatched parenthetic comma
- dangling opener
- passive stacked on passive
- `there is/are` plus an action noun where a strong verb is available
- repeated `the fact that`
- several consecutive sentences with the same connective pattern
- mixed noun/verb/clause items in one list
- `only` or another limiting modifier far from what it limits
- tense drift in summary
- paragraph ending on a trivial detail
- generic approval words standing in for evidence

## Exception Rules Worth Remembering

- Passive voice can be the better choice when the receiver is the topic or the actor is unknown.
- A deliberate fragment can create legitimate emphasis.
- A loose sentence is normal; a long succession of identical loose sentences is the problem.
- Topic-sentence structure is strongest for exposition and argument; narrative and dialogue may work differently.
- A writer may break a rule for a compensating effect, but the effect must be clear enough to justify the cost.

## Modern-Use Gate

**Durable** → apply when context fits: clause punctuation, dangling modifiers, paragraph unity, directness, concreteness, concision, parallelism, modifier proximity, tense control, emphasis.

**Style-dependent** → follow house style: serial comma, quotation and title conventions, reference formatting.

**Historical/dated** → report as Strunk's 1918 prescription unless a period imitation is requested: strict singular-pronoun rules, split-infinitive avoidance, rigid shall/will distinctions, several Chapter V usage bans, and historical hyphenated spellings such as `to-day`.
